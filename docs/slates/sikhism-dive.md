## overview (sourcing: sourced)

Sikhism is the path of the One: the formless, timeless God whom Guru Nanak
proclaimed at the close of the fifteenth century in Punjab, beyond the labels
of Hindu and Muslim. Its roughly twenty-six million adherents make it the
youngest of the world's major religions, and its center holds three
disciplines Nanak taught together: remembrance of the Name, honest work, and
sharing what one earns. The line of ten human Gurus closed in 1708, when
guruship passed to the scripture itself: the Guru Granth Sahib, before which
every Sikh congregation gathers as equals, and beside which every
[[gurdwara]] serves the [[langar]] meal to anyone who comes.

## origins (sourcing: sourced)

Guru Nanak's call came around 1499 at Sultanpur, and his hymns and journeys
gathered the first community at Kartarpur. Nine successors built what he
founded: Angad gave the Gurmukhi script, Amar Das the institutions, Ram Das
the city of Amritsar, and Arjan the Adi Granth and the Harmandir Sahib, before
dying in Mughal custody in 1606, the tradition's first martyr. His son
Hargobind took up two swords, spiritual and temporal; the ninth Guru, Tegh
Bahadur, was executed at Delhi in 1675, in Sikh memory for defending another
community's freedom of conscience. In 1699 Guru Gobind Singh founded the
[[khalsa]], the initiated order bearing the five articles of faith, and before
his death in 1708 sealed the succession forever in the scripture. The
community weathered persecution, won an empire under Ranjit Singh, reformed
itself under the Singh Sabha, and carried the gurdwara across the world.

## core_beliefs (sourcing: sourced)

Ik Onkar: there is one God, creator and pervading presence, beyond form,
gender, and birth, known through grace and remembrance rather than ritual or
renunciation. The world unfolds under hukam, the divine order; the human
disease is haumai, self-centeredness, and its cure is nam simran, remembrance
of the Name, practiced in a householder's life of honest labor and service,
not in withdrawal. All human beings are equal before the One: caste
distinctions are rejected in principle and dissolved in practice at the langar
line. Liberation is union with God, ending the round of rebirth; the Gurus
teach it as the fruit of grace meeting effort, in this life's ordinary duties.

## practices (five entries)

1. nam-simran: Remembrance of the Name, in meditation and through the day.
2. nitnem: The daily discipline of prescribed prayers, morning to night.
3. kirtan: Congregational singing of the Guru Granth Sahib's hymns.
4. seva: Selfless service, from the langar kitchen to the shoe stand, worship
   in the form of work.
5. vand-chhakna: Sharing one's earnings, the third of Nanak's three pillars
   with honest work and remembrance.

## rites (four entries)

1. birth: The child is named from the Guru Granth Sahib, opened at random,
   the first letter of the reading giving the name's initial.
2. coming-of-age: Amrit sanchar, initiation into the Khalsa, taken at any age
   of readiness; the initiated keep the five articles of faith.
3. marriage: Anand karaj, the couple circling the Guru Granth Sahib through
   the four laavan hymns.
4. death: Cremation, with the evening prayer of rest; mourning is restrained
   and the scripture is read in the days after.

## misconceptions

| id | claim | correction |
|---|---|---|
| sikhism-is-a-blend | Sikhism is a blend of Hinduism and Islam. | The tradition understands itself as founded on Nanak's own revelation, and its theology, scripture, and institutions are its own; kinship with its neighbors is not derivation. |
| turban-confusion | The turban marks its wearer as belonging to another faith. | The turban is a Sikh article of faith covering unshorn hair; most turban wearers in Western countries are Sikhs. |
| kirpan-is-a-weapon | The kirpan is a concealed weapon. | It is an article of faith binding its bearer to protect the vulnerable, carried under discipline, not an instrument of aggression. |
