## overview (sourcing: sourced)

Jainism is the path of the conquerors: the jinas, souls who have overcome the
passions and karma that bind all beings, and whose example the tradition
follows toward liberation. Its absolute is [[ahimsa]], nonviolence extended to
every living thing, practiced by some 4.5 million adherents, by India's
census count, through vegetarianism, careful livelihood, and for its monks and
nuns an austerity unmatched among the world's religions. The universe, in Jain
teaching, is uncreated and eternal, and each soul is on its own long road; the
[[tirthankara|tirthankaras]], the twenty-four ford-makers, show the crossing
but cannot carry anyone over. Small in numbers, the community's influence on
Indian ethics, and through Gandhi on the world, has been out of all proportion
to its size.

## origins (sourcing: sourced)

The tradition holds its truth eternal, taught in every cosmic age by
twenty-four tirthankaras; many scholars accept a historical Parshva, the
twenty-third, around the eighth century BCE. Vardhamana Mahavira, the
twenty-fourth, taught across the Gangetic plain in the sixth to fifth
centuries BCE, an elder contemporary of the Buddha in the same renunciant
world, and organized the fourfold community of monks, nuns, laymen, and
laywomen. The great division into Svetambara and Digambara hardened over
centuries, the two differing on monastic dress, the canon's survival, and
women's liberation. Jainism traveled south with Bhadrabahu's migration and
west into Gujarat and Rajasthan, where its temple cities, manuscript
libraries, and merchant communities made it a permanent pillar of Indian
civilization.

## core_beliefs (sourcing: sourced)

Every living thing, human, animal, insect, plant, even beings of earth and
water, houses a jiva, a soul, alike in nature and worth. [[karma]] here is not
metaphor but substance: fine matter drawn to the soul by passion and action,
weighing it into rebirth. Liberation comes by stopping the influx and burning
off the load, through the three jewels of right faith, right knowledge, and
right conduct, and the vows: [[ahimsa]] first, then truthfulness, non-stealing,
chastity, and non-possession, absolute for ascetics and scaled for
householders. [[anekantavada]], many-sidedness, tempers all claims: truth has
more aspects than any single view, and the tradition extends intellectual
nonviolence even to its rivals. There is no creator to petition; the
liberated dwell at the world's summit, perfected and at rest.

## practices (five entries)

1. ahimsa-in-daily-life: Vegetarianism, filtered water, care in walking and
   work: nonviolence as a day's discipline, not a doctrine.
2. samayika: Forty-eight minutes of equanimity, the layperson's daily
   meditation in the ascetic's posture.
3. pratikramana: The confession of harms done, daily and in the great annual
   reckoning.
4. fasting: The tradition's signature austerity, from single-day fasts to
   Paryushana's culminating observances.
5. worship: Puja before the tirthankara's image among Murtipujaks; the
   aniconic orders assemble for scripture and reflection instead.

## rites (four entries)

1. birth: Naming and first outings, kept simply; the tradition's weight falls
   later in life.
2. coming-of-age: Diksha, renunciation into monastic life, taken at any age
   of vocation, is the tradition's great threshold; for most laity, adulthood
   arrives through the vows scaled to household life.
3. marriage: A contract solemnized before community and fire, honored and
   plainly ranked below renunciation.
4. death: Cremation with restraint and recollection; grief is tempered by the
   soul's continuing road.

## misconceptions

| id | claim | correction |
|---|---|---|
| jainism-hindu-sect | Jainism is a sect of Hinduism. | It is an independent tradition of the ancient renunciant world, with its own scriptures, cosmology, and unbroken community, recognized as such in Indian law and scholarship alike. |
| jains-worship-creator | Jains worship Mahavira as a creator god. | There is no creator in Jain cosmology; the tirthankaras are venerated as liberated teachers, exemplars rather than rulers of the world. |
| jainism-impractical | Jain ethics are too extreme to live. | The tradition scales its vows: absolute for ascetics, practicable for householders, who have thrived in commerce and public life for two and a half millennia. |
