## overview (sourcing: sourced)

Shinto is the way of the [[kami]]: the venerated presences of Japan, from the
sun goddess of the imperial line to the spirit of a mountain, a river, a
founding ancestor, or an extraordinary life. It has no founder, no canonical
scripture, no salvation doctrine, and no exclusive membership, which is why
this site counts it with a note rather than a number: most Japanese visit
shrines and keep its rites while telling surveys they have no religion. Its
substance is practice, purification, offering, festival, and the marking of
life's passages, and its home is the shrine, some eighty thousand of them,
from Ise's imperial sanctuary to the neighborhood compound behind a single
vermilion gate.

## origins (sourcing: sourced)

Ritual sites and offerings are attested archaeologically from the Yayoi and
Kofun centuries; the practices later called Shinto have no founding moment.
The eighth-century chronicles, the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki, fixed the kami
narratives and the imperial line's descent from Amaterasu, and the Engishiki
codified shrine ritual. For a millennium kami and buddhas were worshipped
together, shrines and temples interwoven, until the Meiji decrees of 1868 tore
them apart, creating an independent Shinto for the first time and then a state
system that framed shrine rites as civic duty. The occupation disestablished
State Shinto in 1945; the shrines regathered voluntarily under the Association
of Shinto Shrines, and the tradition returned to what it had longest been:
local, seasonal, and woven into ordinary life.

## core_beliefs (sourcing: sourced)

The kami are not omnipotent creators but presences, in the world and of it,
responsive to sincerity and offended by pollution. Purity and impurity, not
sin and salvation, order the tradition's map: death, blood, and disorder
pollute; water, salt, and rite purify, and purification, harae, is the
tradition's central act. The prized disposition is makoto, sincerity: uprightness
of heart before the kami and one's community. Blessing is sought in this
world, harvest, health, passage, prosperity, and the dead become ancestral
presences honored by the household. Myth is told, not enforced: the chronicles
ground the rites without functioning as dogma, and belief follows practice
rather than preceding it.

## practices (five entries)

1. shrine-visiting: Through the torii, water at the basin, offering, two
   bows, two claps, one bow: the everyday grammar of the tradition.
2. harae: Purification by water, salt, and the priest's wand, before rites
   and after pollution.
3. offerings: Food, sake, and the sakaki branch presented to the kami,
   at household kamidana and shrine alike.
4. matsuri: The shrine festival, when the kami processes through its
   neighborhood in the mikoshi and the community feasts it.
5. omamori: Amulets, votive tablets, and fortunes: portable blessing and
   petition, renewed with the year.

## rites (four entries)

1. birth: Hatsumiyamairi, the newborn's first shrine presentation, about a
   month after birth.
2. childhood: Shichi-go-san: children of seven, five, and three brought in
   November to give thanks for growth.
3. marriage: The shrine wedding before the kami, sake shared in three sets of
   three sips.
4. death: Funerals are almost always Buddhist, and shrines avoid death's
   pollution; Shinto's own rites accompany life, and its dead are honored as
   household ancestors.

## misconceptions

| id | claim | correction |
|---|---|---|
| shinto-membership | Shinto is an organized religion one joins. | It is a practice woven into place and season; most participants claim no religious affiliation, and the shrine asks none. |
| shinto-equals-nationalism | Shinto is essentially nationalist State Shinto. | The state system lasted from 1868 to 1945, one chapter in a tradition many centuries older and, for most of its life, local and devotional. |
| torii-decoration | Torii gates are decorative architecture. | The gate marks the boundary into a kami's presence; passing it is entering sacred ground. |
