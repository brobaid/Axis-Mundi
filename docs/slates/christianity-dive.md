## overview (sourcing: sourced)

Christianity holds that the one God is [[trinity|Trinity]], Father, Son, and
Holy Spirit, and that in Jesus of Nazareth the Son became human, was crucified
under Pontius Pilate, and rose from the dead, opening salvation to the world.
That confession, elaborated through creeds, sacraments, and scriptures, has
become the largest religious tradition on earth: 2.3 billion adherents across
a breadth of forms unmatched by any other faith, from the Ethiopian highlands'
ancient liturgies to Pentecostal congregations a century old, with its
demographic center now in the global South. Its branches divide over authority,
sacraments, and [[grace]], and the museum's tree renders those divisions; what
unites them is the person at the center and the claim, according to Christian
faith, that his resurrection is the hinge of history.

## origins (sourcing: sourced)

Christianity begins as a movement within Judaism. Jesus of Nazareth taught,
healed, and proclaimed the kingdom of God in Roman Galilee and Judea until his
crucifixion around 30 CE; his followers' conviction that God had raised him
became the founding faith. Paul of Tarsus and the first missionaries carried
that faith across the Greek-speaking Mediterranean, and the Council of
Jerusalem's ruling that gentiles need not keep the whole Mosaic law opened it
beyond its first people; over the following century the church and rabbinic
Judaism became distinct religions in the long parting of the ways. Three
centuries of intermittent persecution ended with Constantine's toleration in
313 and Theodosius' establishment in 380, while the councils from Nicaea
onward fixed the creed's language for God as Trinity and Christ as fully
divine and fully human. By the time the western empire fell, the church was
the ark that carried classical civilization into the Middle Ages.

## core_beliefs (sourcing: sourced)

God is one being in three persons, and Jesus Christ is the second of them
incarnate: truly God and truly human, in the definition of Chalcedon. His
death, in Christian teaching, deals with sin, and his resurrection defeats
death; salvation is by [[grace]], through the faith and practice whose exact
relation is the Reformation's enduring argument. The church gathers believers
into one body across time, ministering through word and [[sacrament]], with
baptism and the [[eucharist|Eucharist]] held everywhere central and their
number and meaning divided by branch. Scripture is God's word, read alone or
with tradition's authority depending on the branch. Christian hope is
resurrection and a renewed creation, and the ethic given to live toward it is
the double command: love of God with all one's being, and of neighbor as
oneself.

## practices (five entries, in this order)

1. worship: Weekly congregational worship on Sunday, the day of resurrection,
   eucharistic or preaching-centered by branch, through a liturgical year.
2. eucharist: The meal of bread and wine instituted at the Last Supper,
   received as sacrament; its frequency and meaning divide the branches.
3. prayer: From the Lord's Prayer, taught by Jesus himself, to fixed daily
   offices and free extemporary prayer.
4. scripture: Reading and preaching of the Bible, privately and in assembly;
   the sermon is central across Protestantism.
5. fasting: Seasons of penitence and preparation, Lent above all, kept with
   differing rigor across the branches.

## rites (four entries)

1. birth: Baptism in water in the threefold name; its timing is a defining
   division, administered to infants in most branches and reserved for
   professing believers in the baptist and free-church streams.
2. coming-of-age: Confirmation, or believer's baptism itself, seals mature
   membership where it is practiced.
3. marriage: Vows before God and the congregation; counted a sacrament in
   Catholic and Orthodox teaching.
4. death: The funeral commends the dead to God in hope of resurrection; burial
   and, in most modern branches, cremation are both practiced.

## misconceptions

| id | claim | correction |
|---|---|---|
| bible-decided-at-nicaea | The Council of Nicaea decided which books went into the Bible. | Nicaea addressed the divinity of Christ, not the canon; the New Testament list crystallized gradually and appears in Athanasius' letter of 367, with no council vote creating it. |
| christianity-is-western | Christianity is a Western religion. | It began in Roman Judea, its oldest continuous churches are African and Asian, and most Christians today live in the global South. |
| immaculate-conception-confusion | The immaculate conception means the virgin birth. | The Catholic doctrine concerns Mary's own conception without original sin; the virgin birth of Jesus is a separate teaching. |
