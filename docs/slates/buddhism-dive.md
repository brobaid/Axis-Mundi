## overview (sourcing: sourced)

Buddhism is a path rather than a creed about God: the way taught by
Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha, for uprooting the craving that binds beings to
suffering and the round of rebirth. Its 324 million adherents by affiliation
understate its reach, since across East Asia it lives inside plural religious
lives that censuses cannot count. From one teacher in fifth-century-BCE India
it became Asia's great missionary religion, carried in three broad vehicles:
the Theravada of Sri Lanka and mainland Southeast Asia, the Mahayana of East
Asia, and the Vajrayana of Tibet and Mongolia, each with its own canon,
practice, and genius, all honoring the same awakening.

## origins (sourcing: sourced)

According to tradition, Siddhartha Gautama, born to rule a small state below
the Himalayas, renounced his inheritance at twenty-nine, and after six years'
search awoke under the Bodhi tree: the Buddha, the awakened one. He taught for
forty-five years across the Gangetic plain, founding the [[sangha]], the
monastic community that preserved his discourses; scholarly dating centers his
death on the 480s BCE. Ashoka's third-century patronage carried the dharma
across India and beyond, to Sri Lanka where the Pali Canon was written down,
along the Silk Road into China by the first century CE, onward to Korea and
Japan, and over the Himalayas into Tibet. In the land of its birth it faded by
the thirteenth century, to return in the twentieth; everywhere else it became
native, and in the modern West it has planted its newest communities.

## core_beliefs (sourcing: sourced)

The four noble truths frame everything: suffering pervades conditioned
existence; craving drives it; it can end; and the eightfold path, right view
through right concentration, is the way to its ending, [[nirvana]]. All
conditioned things are impermanent, unsatisfactory, and without self:
what wanders rebirth is not a soul but a stream of conditions, arising
dependently. Ethics, meditation, and wisdom train that stream; compassion
grows with insight, and in the Mahayana the [[bodhisattva]]'s vow to awaken
for all beings becomes the ideal itself. The traditions differ over how many
buddhas aid the path and how much help they give, from the Theravada's sober
reverence to Pure Land reliance on Amitabha's vow, and the museum's tree
carries those differences.

## practices (five entries)

1. meditation: The tradition's laboratory: calm and insight practice, Zen's
   sitting, the Vajrayana's visualizations.
2. offerings: Flowers, incense, and light before the image, honoring the
   awakened and recollecting impermanence.
3. chanting: Recitation of refuge, precepts, and scripture, the daily
   liturgy of monastery and home.
4. dana: Giving, above all the lay gift of food and requisites to the sangha,
   the engine of merit.
5. uposatha: The observance days of the lunar fortnight, kept with precepts,
   teaching, and renewal.

## rites (four entries)

1. birth: Blessing ceremonies for the newborn vary by culture; monks chant
   protection and the child is presented at the temple.
2. coming-of-age: Ordination, permanent or temporary: in Theravada lands a
   season in robes remains a young man's rite of passage.
3. marriage: Blessed by monks but civil in nature; the tradition treats it as
   a lay matter, not a sacrament.
4. death: The tradition's central rite: chanting, merit transferred to the
   dead, and in Tibetan and Chinese practice the forty-nine days of passage.

## misconceptions

| id | claim | correction |
|---|---|---|
| buddha-is-a-god | Buddhists worship the Buddha as a god. | The Buddha is honored as the awakened teacher, not a creator; how much aid awakened beings give differs by branch, and none makes him God. |
| buddhism-is-philosophy | Buddhism is a philosophy, not a religion. | It has monastics, ritual, scripture, festivals, and devotion; the philosophy is real, and so is the religion around it. |
| nirvana-is-annihilation | Nirvana means annihilation. | It is the end of craving and the round of rebirth; the tradition itself refuses to describe it as either existence or extinction. |
