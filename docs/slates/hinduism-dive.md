## overview (sourcing: sourced)

Hinduism has no founder, no single scripture, no central authority, and no
requirement that its 1.2 billion adherents believe alike; it is a family of
traditions grown on Indian soil across four millennia, held together by shared
concepts, [[dharma]], [[karma]], [[samsara]], [[moksha]], shared texts, and
shared practice more than by creed. A Hindu may worship Vishnu, Shiva, or the
Goddess, pursue the divine through knowledge, devotion, action, or discipline,
and understand ultimate reality as personal, impersonal, or both: the
tradition's own formula is that truth is one and the wise call it by many
names. It is the world's third-largest religion and its oldest major living
tradition in continuous practice.

## origins (sourcing: sourced)

The oldest layer is the Rigveda, composed in the northwest around 1500-1200
BCE and transmitted orally with astonishing fidelity. The Upanishads, from
about 800 BCE, turned the ritual tradition inward toward [[brahman]] and
[[atman]], the questions all later Hindu philosophy works; the epics and the
Bhagavad Gita brought the teaching into narrative and devotion. In the first
millennium CE, temple worship guided by the Puranas displaced Vedic sacrifice
as the mainstream, and the bhakti movements, beginning among the Tamil poets
and sweeping north over a thousand years, made loving devotion the tradition's
dominant voice. Vedanta's teachers, Shankara, Ramanuja, and Madhva, fixed its
classical philosophies, and the nineteenth century's reformers carried
Hinduism into the modern world and the world beyond India.

## core_beliefs (sourcing: sourced)

The self, [[atman]], is not the body and does not die with it; it wanders
[[samsara]] under the law of [[karma]], and the tradition's shared aim is
[[moksha]], release. What release means divides the schools: identity with
[[brahman]] in Shankara's non-dualism, eternal communion with a personal God
in the devotional Vedantas. [[dharma]] orders life in the meantime: duty
fitted to one's station and stage, with nonviolence, truthfulness, and
hospitality broadly enjoined. The divine is approached through chosen forms,
the ishta-devata, without denying the others; four recognized paths, knowledge,
devotion, action, and meditative discipline, lead to the same end, and most
Hindus walk several at once.

## practices (five entries)

1. puja: Worship of the deity with offerings, light, and mantra, at the home
   shrine daily and in the temple.
2. darshan: Seeing and being seen by the deity's image, the heart of temple
   visiting.
3. yoga: The disciplines of body, breath, and mind, from Patanjali's path to
   modern practice.
4. pilgrimage: Journeys to the tirthas, the fords between worlds, from local
   shrines to the great gatherings at the rivers.
5. vrata: Vowed fasts and observances, kept especially by women, binding
   household life to the sacred calendar.

## rites (four entries)

1. birth: Namakarana, the naming ceremony, among the first of the samskaras.
2. childhood: Upanayana, the sacred-thread initiation into Vedic study,
   traditionally for boys of the upper varnas between eight and twelve.
3. marriage: Vivaha around the sacred fire, the seven steps binding the couple.
4. death: Antyeshti, cremation, with the ashes given to a river, the Ganges
   above all, and rites feeding the ancestors.

## misconceptions

| id | claim | correction |
|---|---|---|
| hinduism-is-polytheism | Hindus worship millions of gods as separate deities. | The tradition's many forms are widely understood as faces of one reality; theologies range from strict non-dualism to devotion to one supreme God. |
| caste-is-the-essence | Caste is Hinduism's scriptural essence. | Social hierarchy and the tradition are historically entangled, but reformers from the bhakti poets to Gandhi have contested caste from within, and modern Hindu law rejects exclusion. |
| cow-worship | Hindus worship cows. | The cow is revered and protected as a symbol of life and generosity, not worshipped as a deity. |
